    Can you trust this model? v0.5¶

    Any flexible model will find something in any data. Give a marketing-mix model ten channels and two years of weekly sales and it will happily tell you what each channel returned - even if the sales were random. The fit statistics will look fine. The charts will look convincing. The only way to know whether the model learned something real is to show it data where there is nothing to learn, and check that it notices.

    That is what the platform's null tests do, and why a governance policy can require them before anyone uses a model. The first half of this page explains them without formulas; the second half gives the statistics. The how-to (policies, validations, approvals, evidence packs) is in Validate models with governance policies.

    In plain words¶

    Shuffled results: does the model beat luck?¶

    Take the drivers the model uses - weekly spend, prices - and shuffle them in time, so that each week's spend lands on some other week while sales stay exactly as they were. Now the relationship between spend and sales is destroyed, by construction, but the sales' own trend and season are untouched. Fit the same model to the shuffled data and measure how well it predicts weeks it was not fitted on. Do that twenty or forty times. (A model without drivers - a forecast from the series' own past - is tested on the series itself: its week-to-week changes are shuffled and added up again, which keeps how much it wanders but removes anything predictable.)

    If the model really learned how sales depend on the drivers, it predicts the real held-out weeks much better than any of the shuffled versions predict theirs. If one shuffled fit in twenty does as well as the real one, the model's apparent skill is the kind of skill you get from noise. The check reports this as a p-value: the share of shuffled fits that did at least as well (with a small correction so it is never exactly zero).

    What you see What it means
    "lower than all 39 fits on shuffled results (p = 0.025)" The model's skill is very unlikely to be luck.
    "1 of 20 fits on shuffled results did as well (p = 0.095)" Borderline: real signal, but weak, or too few shuffles to tell.
    "9 of 20 did as well (p = 0.48)" The model is fitting noise. Do not use it to make decisions.

    Drivers are shuffled in blocks (a run of consecutive weeks moves together), so their own flighting patterns survive; only the link between drivers and result is broken. Because the sales are not shuffled, a model that wins only because of a trend or a season does exactly as well on the shuffled copies, and fails the check.

    Persistence does not fool this test. On a series that wanders (a random walk), a model that differences it gets much of its skill from "next week is like this week", but the shuffled fits keep that persistence too (the result is not shuffled), and skill is measured against the null forecast, so only what the drivers add counts. The cookbook's ARIMAX on such a series, with a price driver that really matters, forecasts better than all 20 shuffled fits (p = 0.048, the output).

    Fake drivers: does the model credit anything it is given?¶

    Add three drivers that are pure noise - copies of a real spend column scrambled in time, or random numbers with the same average and spread - and fit the model again. A trustworthy model gives them almost nothing: a few hundredths of a percent of sales, with a range that comfortably includes zero.

    If a channel made of random numbers gets 8% of sales, the model hands out credit too easily, and the credit it gave your real channels is suspect too. The usual causes are too many drivers for the amount of history, priors that are too loose, or drivers that move together.

    Shifted drivers: is it the timing, or just the season?¶

    Move every driver six months away from when it actually happened and refit. Real advertising effects should vanish - spend in March cannot drive sales in September. If the model still finds most of the effect, it is picking up the season or the trend, and calling it advertising.

    The other checks, briefly¶

    • Beats naive: on weeks it never saw, the model must be better than "same as last week" and "same as this week last year". A forecast that cannot beat that is not worth its complexity.
    • Nothing left in the errors: what the model gets wrong should look like noise; a pattern in the mistakes means the model missed something predictable.
    • Stable over time: refitted on different stretches of history, the key numbers should not swing wildly.
    • Signs make sense: spend should not reduce sales; prices should not raise demand (unless you say so).
    • Assumptions do not decide the answer (Bayesian models): widen and narrow the priors; the answer should barely move.
    • Finds known answers: simulate data from the fitted model, refit, and check that the ranges contain the truth about 90% of the time.
    • The fit finished properly, the error on unseen data is below your limit, and the data is good enough (no long gaps, no constant drivers, no negative spend).

    What the platform does with the results¶

    A policy lists the checks a kind of model must pass, how strict each one is (blocking stops validation, warning is shown to the reviewer, info is evidence only), and whether a second person must approve. When you validate a model version, the checks run in the sandbox, every result is stored with its evidence, and the version becomes validated, awaiting_approval or checks_failed. Endpoints can be told to serve only validated versions. Everything is in the evidence pack an auditor can read without access to the platform.

    mapi validate model model://growth/geo-mmm@v7     # runs the checks, shows every result
mapi approve "$VALIDATION" --comment "Null tests convincing"   # a second person
mapi validations pack "$VALIDATION" --out evidence.html

    For statisticians¶

    Notation: y the target over n periods, ŷ the model's predictions, H the held-out periods of the model's data split, L the check's loss (RMSE by default). Every refit uses the model's own spec and pipeline through the SDK harness (the same code path as a fit), under a refit budget (RefitBudget: at most max_refits refits; Bayesian refits are capped at 200 draws and 200 tuning steps on 2 chains).

    null.permutation_target - a permutation test with a matching null¶

    The statistic is scale-free: the held-out skill of a refit against the null forecast on the same dataset, T = 1 − L(model) / L(null forecast), where the null forecast is the last training value of an integrated series (a random walk) and the training mean otherwise. Raw held-out errors are not comparable across shuffles of the result: their spread comes from which levels a shuffle lands in the test periods, not from the model (in simulation, a level-shuffling test with raw RMSE rejected 35% of random walks and 33% of real AR alternatives - no size and no power).

    The null depends on what the model is meant to have learned:

    • Models with drivers (ARIMAX, marketing mix, regressions on time): y is left alone and the drivers are block-permuted jointly (ℓ = max(1, n // 20) periods per block; the block parameter changes it). H0: the drivers carry no information about y beyond the model's trend, season, controls and dynamics. A model whose refit ignores the drivers (the first shuffled fit scores exactly the same) is tested as a model without drivers.
    • Models without drivers (ARIMA, ETS, Theta, UC): the d-th differences of y are permuted i.i.d. and re-integrated from the original first values (d from the model's ARIMA order, else 1 when ADF keeps a unit root or KPSS rejects stationarity). This keeps the integration order and the distribution of the increments but destroys predictable dynamics; in simulation (ARIMA(1,1,0), 12 held-out periods, B = 19) it keeps its size on random walks and rejects AR dynamics far more often.
    • Data without a time order: the classic row permutation of y, on the held-out loss.

    With T_b the statistic of the b-th shuffled refit, b = 1..B, the Monte-Carlo p-value is

    p = (1 + #{b : T_b >= T0}) / (B + 1)

    which is exact - valid at every B - under exchangeability. The smallest attainable p is 1 / (B + 1): with the default B = 20 it is 0.048, so a 5% test is barely possible. Use B >= 39 for a 2.5% resolution (the policy parameter is n_permutations).

    Thresholds are on p: pass ≤ 0.05, warn ≤ 0.10, fail above.

    null.noise_regressors - placebo regressors measured by ablation¶

    K placebo drivers (n_noise, default 3) are added - a real driver block-permuted in time (kind: shuffled_driver, which keeps its distribution and short-run autocorrelation) or Gaussian noise with its mean and variance - as new channels of a marketing-mix model, or new variables of other models. Placebos get the model's default prior: a real channel's per-channel prior or calibration (a business-prior or lift-test ROI) is never copied to them, or a well-calibrated model would fail because of its prior; carry-over and saturation shapes are copied. After the refit each placebo's effect share is measured through the score path, not read from a coefficient, as a share of the real drivers' total effect:

    s_k = Σ_t |ŷ_t(full) - ŷ_t(placebo k off)|  /  Σ_t |ŷ_t(full) - ŷ_t(real drivers off)|

    (of Σ_t |ŷ_t(full)| when the drivers' effect cannot be measured), so it works for non-linear models (saturation, adstock, trees) alike and does not depend on the size of the baseline. The check reports max_k s_k (pass ≤ 2%, warn ≤ 5%). For Bayesian models a placebo is flagged when its share is above the warn limit with at least 90% posterior probability, P(s_k > 2%) ≥ 0.9 per draw: a "90% interval excludes zero" rule cannot work for a marketing mix model, whose channel effects are positive in every draw. For frequentist models placebo p-values are judged with Holm's correction for the K placebos (uncorrected, a good model is flagged about once in seven runs with K = 3). A flag turns a nominal pass into a warning (a failure when the share is above the fail limit). Models whose in-sample predictions follow the stored history (the statsmodels plugins) use |β_k| · Σ_t |z_k,t − z_k,t(off)| instead.

    null.placebo_time_shift¶

    Each driver is circularly shifted by every δ in shifts (default ±26 periods) and the model refitted; the statistic is the placebo effect as a share of the real effect, max_δ Σ|Δ_δ ŷ| / Σ|Δ ŷ| (pass ≤ 0.25, warn ≤ 0.5). It targets confounding by seasonality and trend: a half-year shift deliberately misaligns annual seasons, so an effect that survives it is not the driver's.

    baseline.beats_naive - out-of-sample skill¶

    On H, with the training-only fit, skill = 1 − MAE(model) / min_j MAE(benchmark_j) over naive (last value), seasonal naive (same period last season), mean and drift forecasts made from the training history; MASE is reported as evidence. Pass ≥ 0.05, warn ≥ 0. For a formal test of the difference on backtest folds, use the compare.diebold_mariano workflow node:

    d_t  = L(e1_t) - L(e2_t)                       (squared or absolute loss)
DM   = mean(d) / sqrt(lrv(d) / n)              lrv: autocovariances up to lag h - 1
HLN  = DM * sqrt((n + 1 - 2h + h(h - 1)/n) / n)
p    = 2 * P(T_{n-1} > |HLN|)

    With one h-step error per backtest fold and folds step periods apart, only ceil(h / step) − 1 neighbouring folds share test periods, so that many autocovariances enter lrv (and h in the correction is replaced by that number plus one); the node reads the spacing from the folds. It needs more paired errors than that; with too few folds it says so instead of returning a number.

    residuals.whiteness¶

    Ljung-Box Q = n(n + 2) Σ_{k=1..m} ρ̂_k² / (n − k) on the in-sample residuals of every series, with the start-up residuals of a diffuse-initialised fit removed (differencing, unobserved components: the fit's own burn-in) and the degrees of freedom reduced by the ARMA terms only (m − p − q − P − Q, never the constant, drivers or variance); the smallest p-value across series counts (pass ≥ 0.05, warn ≥ 0.01). Mind the multiplicity: with many series some rejections are expected, so a panel-wide policy may prefer severity: warning.

    stability.rolling_params¶

    The model is refitted on rolling windows of history (window, default about half the sample); for each key estimate the coefficient of variation across windows is reported, scaled by the estimate's own standard error when the estimate is near zero (otherwise a coefficient that wobbles around zero would look unstable by construction). Pass ≤ 25%, warn ≤ 50%.

    sensitivity.prior and recovery.simulated¶

    Prior sensitivity writes out every prior the model uses - its settings with the plugin's defaults (for mmm.bayesian the default ROI prior LogNormal(0, 1), seasonality.prior_sigma, trend.growth_prior_sigma) and business priors translated at fit time - multiplies every prior width (sigma, scale, sd, tau under a prior… key, and keys named …prior_sigma or …_sigma; never Beta or Gamma beta/b parameters) by each of scales, and reports the largest relative move of a key posterior mean (pass ≤ 20%). Recovery is a parametric-bootstrap version of simulation-based calibration: y* = E[y] + ε* with block-bootstrapped residuals, S refits, and the coverage of the 90% intervals for the fitted values (target 90%, pass within 10 points). It is skipped for autoregressive models, where resampled residuals do not reproduce the model's own dynamics.

    Interpreting a set of results¶

    A policy is a multiple-testing procedure: with a dozen checks at their own thresholds, a sound model will warn on something now and then. That is why severities exist. Make the checks that protect the decision blocking (for a marketing-mix model the null tests and the signs, for a forecast the naive benchmark and holdout accuracy) and leave diagnostics as warning or info for the reviewer. Each CheckResult carries value, threshold, message_plain, an expert message, a suggested_action and the evidence (the real score next to every shuffled score, each placebo's share and interval), so the reviewer can see how close a call was.

    Try it¶

    mapi governance checks                        # what a policy can require
mapi governance checks --about null.permutation_target --expert
mapi governance policies ls                   # in force here: tenant-wide first
mapi governance policies create forecast-policy.yaml
mapi validate model model://growth/wf-weekly-readiness-fit@v1   # runs the checks, waits
mapi validations ls --queue                   # reviewers: what waits for a decision
mapi approve "$VALIDATION" --comment "Null tests convincing; beats naive by 25%"
mapi validations pack "$VALIDATION" --out evidence.html          # for the auditor

    import yaml

with open("forecast-policy.yaml", encoding="utf-8") as fh:
    policy = api.governance.create_policy(yaml.safe_load(fh))
print(policy.id, policy.version, [c.check for c in policy.checks])

# edit and save the next version (refused with 409 if someone saved one meanwhile)
stricter = api.governance.update_policy(
    policy.id, policy.model_copy(update={"approvals_required": 2}), expected_version=1
)
for p in api.governance.policies():  # in force in the project: tenant-wide first
    print(p.scope, p.name, f"v{p.version}")

    validation = api.models.validate(model_id, version, note="Q4 refresh", wait=True)
print(validation.status.value)  # validated, awaiting_approval or checks_failed
for r in validation.results:
    print(r.check, r.status.value, r.message_plain, r.suggested_action or "")
if validation.status.value == "checks_failed":
    validation = api.validations.resubmit(validation.id, note="refitted on clean data")

    # A project policy for the forecasts this team publishes (validated by a docs test).
# `mapi governance policies create forecast-policy.yaml`, or
# `api.governance.create_policy(yaml.safe_load(open("forecast-policy.yaml")))`.
name: Forecasts we publish
description: Every forecast must beat naive, survive the null tests and be signed off.
applies_to: {families: [ts]}
checks:
  - check: baseline.beats_naive                  # better than "same as last period"
  - check: null.permutation_target               # better than on shuffled results
    params: {n_permutations: 39}                 # smallest possible p = 1/40 = 0.025
  - check: null.noise_regressors                 # fake drivers made of noise get ~0 credit
    params: {n_noise: 3}
  - check: residuals.whiteness                   # no pattern left in the errors
    severity: warning
  - check: holdout.accuracy                      # MAPE on unseen weeks
    thresholds: {warn: 0.10, fail: 0.20}
  - check: stability.rolling_params
    severity: info
require_approval: true                           # a second person signs off
approvals_required: 1
require_validated_for_promotion: true            # endpoints refuse unvalidated versions

    A workflow can run the same checks as a node (governance.policy with submit: true) and record the validation itself - see the cookbook.
